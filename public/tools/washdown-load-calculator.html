Guidance & References

Method 1 — Residual Film (average)

After washdown, a thin film of water clings to surfaces and evaporates over the drydown period. Estimate the film thickness left after it stops draining, multiply by wetted area to get pounds of water, and divide by drydown time for the average rate. Use this to size for recovering the space after each washdown.

Method 2 — Peak Evaporation Rate (Carrier)

While surfaces are fully wet, evaporation runs at its peak. The Carrier equation gives that instantaneous rate, driven by warm water, high airflow, and dry target air. Use it when RH must be held during and immediately after the washdown — it is the more conservative number.

E = A × (95 + 0.425 V) × (Pw − Pa) ÷ 1050   ·   Y = 1050 Btu/lb latent heat

Important: the peak rate only lasts until the residual film evaporates (shown above in minutes). Because there is only a small, finite amount of water, that window is usually short. Sizing on the raw peak can badly oversize the unit — many designs size on the Method 1 recovery basis and accept a brief RH excursion during the wash. Use the peak when a short excursion is unacceptable for a sensitive product or process.

Typical residual film thickness

  • 0.003 in — smooth sealed floor, good slope, squeegeed
  • 0.005 in — sealed floor, fair drainage (good default)
  • 0.010 in — textured surface, average drainage
  • 0.015–0.020 in — rough, porous, or poorly drained surfaces

Key conversions (IAT framework)

grains/hr = lb/hr × 7,000   |   gallons = lb ÷ 8.34   |   sea-level barometric pressure = 29.921 in. Hg

Applying the result

Add the washdown load to the space's other loads — vapor permeation, infiltration and door openings, fresh-air makeup, people, and product — to get the total the desiccant unit must remove. The Design Point above does this once you enter your other-loads total.
